# Consumer-goods drop: registrations, review and a ballot by shoe size

**Recorded:** 16 October 2026. **Status:** discovery proposal with a working synthetic demo; no live integration.
**Example:** Vans x Satoshi Nakamoto Era “Gems” / “Lucky Charms”. Vans is an
illustrative case, not a customer, sponsor or confirmed partner. This extends the
same decision-and-access hypothesis as the ticket pilot without changing the
[Fi demo scope](./fi-demo-2026-10-23.md).

## Problem and evidence

A limited release rewards speed, repeated requests and multiple entries when
access depends on reaching checkout first. Shoppers can encounter errors, unclear
stock and failed purchases. Site availability and fair allocation are separate
problems; neither a ballot nor an AI conversation replaces traffic protection.

## How people and agents gain an advantage

The relevant threat is extra opportunities or rule-breaking, not automation alone.
A legitimate agent may monitor official releases, prepare one authorised account,
submit one permitted entry, track an outcome and help complete an authorised
purchase. Assistive input should not reduce that shopper's chance.

| Technique | Human version | Automation adds | Pilot response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Preparation | Release alerts, sign-in, autofill and ready payment | Reliable monitoring and routine completion | Remove speed advantage through a window; permit legitimate assistance |
| Multiple entries | Extra accounts or false identities | Cheap entry multiplication | Cross-pool identity links, duplicate resolution and one permitted entry |
| Coordinated buyers | Friends or paid helpers acting for one buyer | Coordination across many accounts | Review contextual patterns; distinct people do not prove independent beneficiaries |
| Parallel queue positions | Several sessions/devices | Many simultaneous attempts | Specialist queue/session controls; a queue is not a personhood check |
| Fast checkout and retries | Express payment and repeated attempts | Immediate reactions and persistent retries | Reserved windows, single-use enforcement and order reconciliation |
| Several retailers | Enter separate permitted draws | Wider monitoring | Not automatically abuse; respect each retailer's rules |
| Inventory capture | Hold carts or reservations without completing | Repeated reservation at scale | Bounded expiry, quantity limits and same-size waitlist release |

These are general patterns, not findings about the Vans incident. The FTC describes
allegations involving automated reservations, fictitious accounts and concealed
network identities in [ticket-limit enforcement](https://search.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/01/ftc-brings-first-ever-cases-under-bots-act).
[Nike's fairness explanation](https://www.nike.com/launch/t/inside-snkrs-fairness)
also addresses multiple-account behaviour. Ticket enforcement is evidence of a
broader pattern, not a claim about sneaker law or this retailer.

A goal-only agent could attempt to multiply identities or tailor convincing answers
to published selection criteria. This is threat-model reasoning, not a measured
claim about any specific agent. General-purpose agents are not necessarily faster
than specialised checkout software. Do not try to resolve this by scoring writing
style or asking an LLM whether an applicant sounds human.

An equal-entry ballot is not automatically an equal-person ballot: with 100 accepted
entries and 10 winners, one entry has a 10% chance; controlling ten entries gives
about a 67% chance of at least one win in that fixed pool (sampling without
replacement). Cross-size entries also create extra opportunities unless identity
constraints span the whole drop. A real human group buying for one reseller remains
harder to establish than an exact duplicate, and may remain unresolved.

## Pilot proposition

**Let shoppers register over a defined window, help the brand review suspicious
registration clusters, then allocate equal-chance purchase opportunities against
available stock in each size.**

Groucho would own the registration/review/allocation workflow. A commerce partner
would retain inventory, payment, order creation, fulfilment and checkout enforcement.
Specialist edge protection and capacity controls would protect the infrastructure.
This is a proposal to test, not a claim that these integrations already exist.

## Shopper and operator workflow

1. **Register:** open an accessible registration window, for example 24 hours.
   Collect a verified contact, one selected size/variant and acceptance of the
   disclosed rules. Registration is not a purchase or guaranteed reservation.
   Edits before close replace the existing choice rather than creating an entry.
   Speed within the window does not improve odds.
2. **Inspect:** show verification status, exact duplicate submissions and observed
   relationships across entries. Group suspicious patterns with inspectable
   reasons, their sources and uncertainty, rather than a single “bot score”.
3. **Review:** operators can clear a weak flag, hold a case, request an alternative
   check or resolve a duplicate with a recorded reason. Filters only change the
   view. Explicit eligibility decisions determine the ballot pool.
4. **Freeze:** close registration, complete review, reconcile available sellable
   stock, and preview counts/exclusions per size. Freeze eligible entry IDs,
   selected variants, policy, inventory quantities, time and ballot version.
5. **Allocate:** independently shuffle each size pool, select up to its available
   quantity without replacement and retain the remaining order as its waitlist.
   One entry can win at most one pair across the whole drop.
6. **Offer:** notify selected shoppers of a time-limited purchase opportunity,
   bound to their authenticated account and allocated variant. Reserve the pair
   only through the commerce system's confirmed stock controls.
7. **Recycle:** when a reservation expires or is definitively released, offer it
   to the next eligible shopper on the same size's waitlist. Keep payment retries
   and order reconciliation safe from duplicate offers or charges.

The initial pilot should permit one size choice per applicant, not multiple size
entries or fallback sizes. This simplifies the policy and prevents cross-pool
advantages. Shared households need an exception/review policy; a shared address or
network alone does not establish duplicate people. Account verification does not
prove universal one-person uniqueness.

## Shopper journey: from discovery to purchase

1. **Discover:** a branded page explains the product, price, size system, deadline,
   one-entry policy and result timing. “Arriving earlier does not improve your
   chances.” The demo uses text-only illustrative Vans framing, no brand assets.
2. **Register:** choose one size, provide contact details and establish account
   control. “Registration is not a purchase or a guaranteed reservation.” A live
   product should allow edits before close without creating another entry.
3. **Clarify if needed:** most shoppers finish after verification. An unresolved
   case receives a specific additional-check request, deadline and accessible
   alternative; private profiles or missing history are not automatic failures.
4. **Check status:** a simple status page confirms completion and result timing.
   Operators resolve exceptions before freezing the eligible pools by size.
5. **Receive an outcome:** a selected shopper gets a reserved opportunity with an
   explicit expiry; an eligible non-winner joins the same-size waitlist; an excluded
   shopper receives the reason and the available review route.
6. **Buy calmly:** authenticate as the selected recipient and purchase the fixed
   allocated size during the reservation window. No second checkout race. The store
   handles payment, delivery details and a definitive order confirmation.
7. **Release unclaimed pairs:** only after an expired/declined reservation is
   definitively released does the next shopper in that size receive an offer.
   Reconcile uncertain payments before recycling inventory.

## Suspicious-cluster examples

| Synthetic case | Evidence to show | Review response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sequential email pattern | Related address templates and, if actually collected, submission timestamps | Investigate together; pattern alone does not prove abuse |
| Repeated verified identifier | Same account/control identifier attached to several entries | Resolve duplicate/conflicting entries before freeze |
| Innocent concentration | Several real shoppers using a university domain or household network | Clear with a reason; no automatic domain/IP rejection |
| Privacy-conscious newcomer | Private social profile, no purchase history, verified alternative | Keep a route to eligibility; no popularity or loyalty requirement |
| Incomplete check | Contact control not completed | Keep pending until the stated deadline; explain recovery |

Do not universally strip email dots/plus tags: alias semantics vary by provider.
Do not require broad social searches, sensitive-trait inference, eloquent answers
or a paid purchase history for the general pool. Fast completion or agent-assisted
input is not itself abuse. World ID and social-control checks are optional future
adapters, not prerequisites for this pilot. Any conversation should serve a clear
brand/research purpose, remain proportionate and not determine general-pool odds.

### Review scenarios in the updated sandbox

Use the scenario shortcuts in Applicant review:

- **Duplicate across sizes:** Alex Morgan (UK 6) and Jules Ward (UK 7) have the
  same explicit simulated verified-person identifier. Both are excluded while
  unresolved. Clearing both does not create two eligible entries. Hold Jules with
  a reason to retain Alex's single original entry. The identity is a fixture fact,
  not a match guessed from names, IP addresses or email appearance.
- **Shared household:** Casey Patel (UK 7) and Riley Patel (UK 8) share a synthetic
  delivery household/network but have distinct simulated person identifiers. Both
  start eligible. Their contextual relationship must not merge their identities.
- **Possible coordinated group:** Eden, Kit and River have a timed address-pattern
  link across sizes 8–10 but distinct simulated identifiers. Open each related
  applicant, record a reason and clear, hold or keep pending individually. Clearing
  one never clears the group. “Keep pending / request clarification” records a
  review state; it sends nothing. Unresolved entries remain outside the frozen
  pool, without being labelled proven bots or resellers.

Show the contrast explicitly: exact fixture identity links justify duplicate
resolution; a shared household alone does not; correlated patterns require human
judgment and may never establish a common beneficiary. In a live pilot, additional
checks and deadlines need an accessible response and appeal process. No identity
provider, network collection or address collection has been added to the demo.

## What “fair ballot by size” means

- Equal chance within each size pool; chances differ between sizes because demand
  and stock differ. In the example, size 6 has a 2/8 chance and size 9 a 2/10 chance.
- No extra entries or weighting for referrals, optional evidence or conversation
  quality. Any separate curated/loyalty allocation must be disclosed and inventory
  separated before the general ballot.
- No silent size changes, cross-size substitutions or transfers between waitlists.
  Unused stock in a size needs a disclosed later-release policy, not an improvised
  rerun that changes the frozen ballot.
- Use cryptographically secure randomness, immutable/versioned input and a durable
  result for a real pilot. An audit log alone is not independent verification.
  Label deterministic rehearsals and simulations explicitly.
- Prevent repeated draw requests, concurrent over-allocation and reuse of purchase
  permissions. Corrections require an explicit documented process, not a hidden
  redraw. Give applicants an understandable review/appeal deadline before freeze.

## Integration boundary and availability

| Groucho proposal | Commerce partner requirement | Infrastructure requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Registration and review | Stable product/variant IDs and authoritative sellable stock | Protected registration endpoints, rate limits and abuse monitoring |
| Size-specific allocation ledger | Atomic reservations, quantity enforcement and expiry/release | Capacity-tested queue and checkout admission |
| Account-bound purchase opportunity | Server validation of recipient, variant, expiry and single use | Protect cart/checkout APIs as well as product pages |
| Waitlist progression | Idempotent order events, reconciliation and failure recovery | Monitoring and graceful overload handling |

A shareable URL or ordinary discount code is not sufficient purchase enforcement.
A standalone review/export prototype can test operator value, but cannot claim to
prevent checkout bypass or preserve inventory without partner enforcement.
Avoid collecting card data: payment remains with the retailer/provider.

For traffic surges, existing products can queue excess visitors before they reach
origin servers: [Cloudflare Waiting Room documentation](https://developers.cloudflare.com/waiting-room/).
A queue alone is not a uniqueness or fairness mechanism; Cloudflare also documents
[bot challenges within waiting rooms](https://blog.cloudflare.com/banish-bots-from-your-waiting-room-and-improve-wait-times-for-real-users/).
Groucho should complement that infrastructure, not claim to replace it. Spreading
registration and checkout over time may reduce synchronised demand, but malicious
traffic can still attack public endpoints.

## Bounded experiment and demo

**Now — synthetic prototype:** a standalone size-pool sandbox is implemented as
described above, reusing the existing secure shuffle. It shows
one suspicious cluster, an innocent case cleared, cohort counts, a frozen draw
and a simulated expiring offer. All provider results, stock and notifications are
fixtures; no brand API, payments or real orders. This does not expand Friday's Ezra demonstration.

**Next — discovery and permissioned trial:** identify a brand/operator sponsor,
review an appropriately handled sample, understand existing tools and measure
manual review. Agree scope, privacy/access/retention rules, exceptions, inventory
ownership and integration/support cost before a paid live pilot. A review-only
trial is a valid earlier step if checkout integration is not yet justified.

**Later — enforced purchase handoff:** integrate reservations, authenticated
permissions, order reconciliation and waitlist release only once the retailer can
support the required controls and their value justifies bespoke delivery.

## Success measures and validation

Compare against an agreed baseline, separating allocation from site availability:

- Operator minutes per reviewed batch; useful flags versus flags cleared as innocent.
- Registration completion, verification failures, support and appeal outcomes.
- Eligible/winner/waitlist accounting per size; duplicate awards, overselling and
  permission reuse must be zero in the controlled pilot.
- Purchase-offer conversion, time to release expired stock and sell-through by size.
- Checkout errors, availability and shopper complaints, measured with the retailer
  and infrastructure provider. Do not attribute improvements to Groucho alone.
- Confirmed coordinated-abuse findings where evidence supports them; neither
  flagged entries nor blocked requests automatically count as prevented scalping.

Meaningful implementation checks should cover per-size eligibility, zero-stock and
undersubscribed pools, one winner per resolved applicant, stock limits, frozen-input
integrity, repeat/concurrent draws, stable waitlists, expired/replayed permissions,
and delayed or duplicate commerce events. Focused model tests cover shared-identifier exclusion, review, verification,
frozen inputs, per-size stock limits, repeat draw/purchase prevention, expiry and
waitlist exhaustion. Production integration checks remain future work.

Discovery must answer who pays, which decision improves, what evidence is available,
whether a ballot suits the brand, which friction is acceptable, and whether an
export is useful before a purchase integration. A retailer's willingness to share
permissioned data and own the checkout boundary is a stronger signal than enthusiasm
for the demo.
